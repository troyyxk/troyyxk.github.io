## The Problem

Generating a video with a diffusion model is expensive. The model starts from noise and denoises the clip step by step, over a long sequence of spatiotemporal tokens, so a single request can keep a GPU busy for seconds to minutes. Serving video generation at scale means paying that cost over and over.

There is an obvious source of savings hiding in the traffic. Real workloads are repetitive: many requests ask for broadly similar things — the same kind of scene, the same objects, the same layout — so the computation behind one finished request could help answer a later one. Reusing work across requests is called **cross-request reuse**.

The hard part is knowing when reuse is *safe*. Existing methods decide it from **prompt similarity**: if the text of the new request is close enough to a past one, reuse its cache. But similar prompts do not guarantee similar videos. Two prompts that read almost the same can drift into completely different visual structure as denoising proceeds, so a cache accepted on prompt similarity alone can quietly carry the wrong scene into the result — and the final quality score may not even reveal it.

That leaves two questions, and Carnator is built around answering both:

<ol class="paper-steps">
  <li><strong>Reuse validity.</strong> Is this historical computation actually compatible with the request we are serving right now?</li>
  <li><strong>Reuse scope.</strong> If it is, which parts of the computation can we skip — and which parts must still be computed for the new request?</li>
</ol>

## Key Idea

Carnator stops guessing from the text and looks inside the model instead.

<div class="paper-callout">
  <span class="paper-callout__label">The insight</span>
  <p>Prompt similarity is only a <em>proxy</em> for compatibility. The generation process itself is the real signal.</p>
  <p>Early in denoising, the partially formed state already reveals how the clip is taking shape — where objects are appearing, how large they are, what they look like. Carnator runs a <strong>lightweight early probe</strong>, summarises that evidence into a compact <strong>Early Signature</strong>, and compares signatures instead of prompts.</p>
  <p>Because the same evidence also marks <em>where</em> the target-specific content sits, it answers the second question too: reuse everywhere the generations agree, and recompute only the regions that genuinely differ.</p>
</div>

## How It Works

![Overview of Carnator](/publications/paper_imgs/2026-09-22-Carnator.png)

*Figure 1: Historical requests are recalled by text, filtered using generation-native Early Signatures, and — for an accepted cache — reused jointly across latent trajectories and sparse attention connectivity, while target-specific computation is preserved.*

<ol class="paper-steps">
  <li><strong>Probe early, not at the end.</strong> A short and cheap probe of the target's early denoising states builds an Early Signature. For each object named in the prompt it records a spatiotemporal support mask, the object's relative size, and its mean CIELAB colour.</li>
  <li><strong>Filter under a risk budget.</strong> The target signature is compared with recalled candidates on four discrepancies — context appearance, object size, spatiotemporal shape, and object appearance. The thresholds are calibrated to maximise reuse coverage subject to a bound on unsafe reuses, then certified on a held-out set with a one-sided Clopper&ndash;Pearson bound. If nothing passes, the request simply falls back to full generation.</li>
  <li><strong>Reuse along two axes at once.</strong> Once a cache is accepted, <em>region-guided latent reuse</em> recomputes tokens inside the evidence-derived active region and inherits the rest from the historical trajectory, while <em>historical sparse-connectivity reuse</em> decides <em>where</em> attention is evaluated — transferring the historical attention structure, not the historical attention values. The two are combined in the middle Transformer layers.</li>
</ol>

## Results

Evaluated on three text-to-video backbones — Wan2.2-TI2V-5B, Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B and LTX-Video-13B — over a 1,000-request warm cache and a disjoint 300-request stream from VidProM, on NVIDIA H100 GPUs. Every online cost is counted, including retrieval, probing, signature construction, filtering and fallback.

### What the numbers say

<ol class="paper-steps">
  <li><strong>It wins by being pickier, not looser.</strong> Adding the Early Signature lowers the hit rate from 77.33% to 72.67%: roughly one in twenty textually similar caches is rejected as generation-incompatible. Latency barely moves (185.6&nbsp;s to 187.30&nbsp;s) while CLIP-T rises from 0.2725 to 0.2913 and VBench-Q from 0.6301 to 0.6382 — the filter removes caches that were hurting alignment, and costs almost nothing.</li>
  <li><strong>Fewer accepted caches, larger savings each.</strong> On Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B, Carnator accepts fewer caches than the baselines yet still delivers the fastest cache-hit latency, dropping it from 1138.6&nbsp;s (dense) to 525.58&nbsp;s. Joint reuse extracts more computation out of each cache it accepts.</li>
  <li><strong>Quality is preserved, not traded away.</strong> On the primary model, VBench-Q is 0.6382 against 0.6331 for dense generation, and CLIP-T stays in the same range as the baselines.</li>
  <li><strong>The two reuse axes are complementary.</strong> On a fixed 100-request subset, latent reuse alone reaches 206.06&nbsp;s and attention reuse alone 160.91&nbsp;s, but combining them reaches 155.85&nbsp;s (1.71×) — better than either on its own.</li>
</ol>

On a disjoint certification set, the risk-aware rule accepts 50 of 70 request-cache pairs with no unsafe cases, giving a one-sided Clopper&ndash;Pearson upper bound of 4.50% and satisfying the prescribed 5% risk constraint.
