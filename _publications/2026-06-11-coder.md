## The Problem

Search engines and retrieval systems rank documents by how semantically similar they are to the query. For most queries that is a reasonable proxy for relevance. For some, it is actively wrong.

Consider a query like *"a quiet hotel for light sleep, a place away from noisy nightlife."* The user is stating a **constraint with a direction**: quiet is what they want, noise is what they want to avoid. Now consider a review that reads *"unlike other quiet hotels, this one is noisy at night."* That review is topically extremely close to the query — same words, same subject — and it argues the exact opposite of what the user asked for. A similarity retriever will rank it highly.

The paper names this failure **constraint-violating evidence exposure**. Similarity measures the topic; it has no notion of which *direction* the evidence points. So the retriever puts documents that violate the user's constraint into the top ranks, where the user actually looks.

Existing remedies sit at two extremes. Logic-based pipelines decompose the query into first-order logic, which needs translation or judging modules and adds latency and deployment complexity when implemented with strong external LLMs. Query-embedding optimisation is lightweight, but it only changes the query representation — the topical embedding space is left as it was, which limits how consistently it can separate satisfying from violating evidence.

## Key Idea

CoDeR separates two things that similarity collapses into one: **topical relevance** and **constraint compatibility**. A document can be highly relevant to the topic and still incompatible with the constraint.

<div class="paper-callout">
  <span class="paper-callout__label">The insight</span>
  <p>Rather than rewriting the query to steer away from violating documents, put the pressure on the evidence itself: learn a representation in which constraint <em>direction</em> is separable from topical relatedness.</p>
  <p>That means training a scorer on explicit satisfying-versus-violating contrasts, and applying it to the topically plausible candidates a normal retriever already returns. The intervention happens on the document side, locally, without any external LLM in the loop.</p>
</div>

## How It Works

![CoDeR overview](/publications/paper_imgs/2026-07-01-CoDeR.png)

*Figure 1: CoDeR keeps a standard topical encoder for candidate coverage and adds a compatibility scorer trained on satisfying-versus-violating contrasts. The two signals are combined into a final ranking.*

<ol class="paper-steps">
  <li><strong>Keep an ordinary topical retriever.</strong> A standard dense encoder still handles candidate coverage, so the system does not lose the recall that makes a retriever usable in the first place.</li>
  <li><strong>Add a compatibility scorer.</strong> A second encoder — a bi-encoder initialised from bge-large-en-v1.5 — is trained to score constraint compatibility separately from topical similarity.</li>
  <li><strong>Train it on lexical polarity contrasts.</strong> Supervision comes in two stages: WordNet-derived word-level lexical polarity triplets, then sentence-level triplets drawn from 800 NevIR and 800 ExcluIR training queries. All queries, triplets and associated documents used to train the scorer are removed from the evaluation splits.</li>
  <li><strong>Combine the two signals.</strong> The compatibility score can either rescore the topical candidates or retrieve an auxiliary compatibility-oriented candidate set, which is how the reported CoDeR-Seq and CoDeR-Union variants differ. Both produce a ranked list using only local encoder scoring — no external LLM calls at inference time.</li>
</ol>

## Results

CoDeR is measured with two diagnostics that are specific to the failure it targets, rather than with general ranking quality alone:

- **V@k** — whether a *violating* document appears in the top k results (lower is better)
- **FVR** — the rank of the first violating document (higher is better)

It is evaluated on three controlled diagnostic sets, one per constraint type (antonymy, negation, exclusion), and on public negative-constraint retrieval benchmarks with stronger baselines including BM25, BGE, Contriever, HyDE, NS-IR and NevIR. All reported numbers are averaged over 10 independent runs and the ranking is stable across them.

### What the numbers say

<ol class="paper-steps">
  <li><strong>The metric that matters is exposure, not average rank quality.</strong> A user reads the first few results. V@k asks whether a document that contradicts the stated constraint is sitting there, which is precisely the failure being studied — and it is invisible to an aggregate similarity score.</li>
  <li><strong>Antonymy and negation are the hard cases.</strong> These are where a document keeps the query's vocabulary while flipping its meaning, so the topical signal actively misleads. The V@2 reductions there (20.6 and 23.5 points) are roughly four times the one on exclusion, where the violating documents are less lexically entangled with the query.</li>
  <li><strong>General retrieval quality is preserved.</strong> The method is also evaluated on BEIR-style benchmarks with nDCG@10 and MAP@10, reported as a preservation check rather than a claim of general-purpose superiority: separating constraint direction should not cost ordinary topical coverage, and it does not.</li>
  <li><strong>The gains come from the representation, not from a cleverer prompt.</strong> Query-rewriting baselines make the query more informative but keep the inherited topical space. CoDeR instead trains on satisfying&ndash;violating contrasts, so the compatibility signal is available for any candidate set at no inference-time API cost.</li>
</ol>
